\begin{abstract}
Detecting people in aerial disaster imagery is constrained by object size, since
at survey altitude a person occupies fewer than ten pixels, below the resolving
limit of a standard detection head. Existing remedies introduce attention
modules, higher-resolution detection scales, sliced inference or state-space
blocks, but each is reported in isolation and on different datasets, so their
relative contribution is unknown. This paper proposes MSA-YOLO, a multi-scale
attention detector in
which every component is selected by evaluating four candidates on one detector
under a single training protocol and preprocessing pipeline. A stride-4
detection scale provides the largest network-side improvement at 1.1 points of
sub-8-pixel recall, an attention substitution contributes a further 0.3 points
while reducing parameter count, and a bidirectional state-space neck yields no
improvement for 2.4 million additional parameters and 2.8 times the latency. The
resulting detector attains 0.853 mean average precision with 19.6 million
parameters, second among published results on the C2A benchmark to a model three
times its size. The largest single improvement originates in training rather
than architecture: a tiny-aware modification to label assignment improves
sub-8-pixel recall by 2.08 points at no inference cost, with a bootstrap interval
excluding zero and a consistent effect across three seeds. A scene-level audit
further establishes that 98 percent of the benchmark test scenes also occur in
training, an inflation of 1.6 points, so this result is measured on a
scene-disjoint re-split. Evaluation on drone and disaster footage collected for
this study yields 0.335 and 0.133 mean average precision without adaptation, and
248 annotated real frames raise these to 0.795 and 0.411 while synthetic
performance is retained at 0.837. No instance below eight pixels is recovered on
real footage.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Survival rates in earthquakes and floods fall sharply with the time taken to
locate casualties, so search speed is an operational constraint rather than a
convenience. Unmanned aerial vehicles can survey a disaster site faster than a
ground team, but a single sortie yields thousands of high-resolution frames in
which each person occupies only a few dozen pixels. Manual review of that volume
is impractical, which motivates automatic detection.

The difficulty is geometric. A one-stage detector predicts boxes on a grid whose
cell covers a stride-sized region of the input, so at the finest standard scale,
stride 8, a twelve-pixel person spans about one and a half cells, and a detector
cannot localize what falls inside a fraction of one cell.
In the C2A disaster benchmark used here~\cite{nihal2024c2a}, 34.5\% of
annotated people are smaller than eight pixels and 63\% smaller than sixteen,
measured on the square root of box area.

Four families of remedy address this regime, reviewed in Section~\ref{sec:rw}.
Each is reported in isolation, on different datasets and without a shared
training protocol, so their relative contribution remains unquantified and a
practitioner choosing among them has little evidence on which to base the
decision.

This paper proposes MSA-YOLO (Multi-Scale Attention YOLO), a detector for this
regime built on YOLO11m~\cite{jocher2024yolo11}, in which each component is
selected by measurement rather than assumption. The name denotes attention on the
deepest features combined with a finer detection scale. Four candidates were evaluated on a single
detector under one fixed protocol: channel-and-spatial attention, a stride-4
detection scale, a bidirectional state-space neck, and the overlap measure used
by the label assigner. Three were retained and one was rejected. The ordering of
that selection constitutes the principal finding of this work, since the largest
contribution to sub-8-pixel recall arises not from the network but from the
assignment of supervision during training. The proposed detector is then
evaluated on drone and disaster footage collected and annotated for this study,
because detectors trained on composited imagery are seldom tested on real
scenes. On that footage no instance below 8 px is recovered.

The contributions of this work are as follows.
\begin{itemize}
\item MSA-YOLO, a detector whose components are selected by single-protocol
measurement, attaining 0.853 mean average precision with 19.6 million parameters
while rejecting a state-space neck that increases latency 2.8-fold without
accuracy benefit.
\item A tiny-aware assignment blend within the task-aligned assigner that
improves sub-8-pixel recall by 2.08 points at no inference cost, with a positive
effect across three training seeds.
\item A scene-level audit of C2A establishing that 98\% of its test scenes also
occur in training, together with a scene-disjoint re-split that quantifies the
resulting inflation.
\item An annotated multi-domain aerial corpus of 248 training and 133 evaluation
frames, and a transfer study quantifying the synthetic-to-real gap and the limits
of a small annotation budget.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:rw}
Object detection divides into two-stage detectors in the R-CNN line, one-stage
detectors such as RetinaNet~\cite{lin2017focal} and the YOLO
family~\cite{wang2024yolov9,jocher2024yolo11}, and transformer designs such as
RT-DETR~\cite{zhao2024rtdetr}. This work adopts the one-stage YOLO family because
its speed and small model size suit airborne deployment.

Detecting very small objects is a distinct problem~\cite{cheng2023smallobject},
addressed in the literature at four points in the pipeline. The first adds
resolution, where feature pyramids and a stride-4 head extend detection to
targets a few pixels wide~\cite{zhu2021tphyolov5}. The second re-weights
features, most often through combined channel-and-spatial
attention~\cite{woo2018cbam}. A third operates at inference time by slicing the
image into overlapping crops~\cite{akyon2022sahi}. The fourth modifies the
training step that selects positive samples, motivated by the observation that
intersection over union collapses for tiny boxes: Wang et
al.~\cite{wang2021nwd} replaced it with a
normalized Wasserstein distance, RFLA~\cite{xu2022rfla} built assignment on a
receptive-field prior, and SimD~\cite{shi2024simd} learned an adaptive
similarity. Closest to the setting here, YOLOv7-UAV~\cite{zeng2023yolov7uav}
weights a Wasserstein term against IoU when allocating positive and negative
samples for UAV imagery, including tiny people, inside an anchor-based
pipeline. Modern YOLO versions instead assign samples with the task-aligned
assigner of TOOD~\cite{feng2021tood}, where the overlap term feeds an alignment
metric that ranks classification and localization jointly, so the same blend
acts on more than a candidate filter. Section~\ref{sec:assign} places it there.
What has not previously been reported is the comparison in
Section~\ref{sec:where}: this component measured against the three architectural
families on one detector under one protocol.

Real search-and-rescue collections such as SARD~\cite{sambolek2021sard} and
HERIDAL~\cite{bozic2019heridal} hold only a few thousand frames.
C2A~\cite{nihal2024c2a} instead composites segmented human figures onto real
disaster backgrounds~\cite{kyrkou2020aider}, giving 10{,}215 images and roughly
360{,}000 instances. Results on such data are almost always in-domain, which
leaves transfer untested. Work on fine-tuning synthetic-trained models with a
little real data~\cite{tremblay2018synthetic} suggests the recipe of
Section~\ref{sec:real}.

\section{Proposed Method}

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centerline{\includegraphics[width=0.92\textwidth]{figures/fig_arch_v2.jpg}}
\caption{The proposed MSA-YOLO pipeline, shown on a C2A scene.}
\label{fig:arch}
\end{figure*}

The full pipeline is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}. The backbone produces
features at five resolutions and the terminal self-attention block is replaced
by CBAM, drawn in orange. The neck fuses those scales and is carried one level
further than the standard design to produce a stride-4 branch, so the head
decodes at strides 4, 8, 16 and 32. The insets along the edges are the model's
own signals on that scene: the input, the attention response, the stride-4
feature response, and the final detections. The stride-4 map responds at the
smallest scattered figures, which the stride-8 level summarizes into single
cells.

MSA-YOLO is a detector for tiny aerial humans built on
YOLO11m~\cite{jocher2024yolo11} with two changes to the network and one to the
way training assigns supervision. Every component was selected by measurement
rather than assumption: each was introduced alone, under one fixed protocol, and
kept only where it earned its cost. Section~\ref{sec:where} reports that
selection, including one candidate that was rejected.

\subsection{Problem Formulation}
Given an aerial image $I$, the detector predicts a set of person boxes with
confidences, $\{(\hat{b}_i, \hat{s}_i)\}$, which is scored against the ground
truth $\{b_j\}$. Training minimizes
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L} = \lambda_{c}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cls}}
              + \lambda_{b}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{box}}
              + \lambda_{d}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{dfl}},
  \label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
where all three terms act only on predictions the assigner has marked positive.
Writing $r_{<8}$ for recall on people below 8 px, the design objective is to
maximize $r_{<8}$ subject to no regression in overall mAP@0.5. Since the
assigner rather than the loss determines which predictions receive supervision,
Section~\ref{sec:assign} modifies the assigner.

\subsection{Corpus Construction and Preprocessing}
\label{sec:corpus}
No public benchmark pairs composited disaster imagery with real footage of the
same task, so a multi-domain corpus was assembled for this study. Aerial video
was recorded with a DJI Air 3S quadrotor, whose 1-inch CMOS main camera captures
3840 $\times$ 2160 at 60 frames per second, flown at 10, 30 and 50 m above
ground level over open ground and over a
university campus where tree canopy occludes many subjects. Disaster footage was
sampled from news video of three events, two supplying training frames and the
third reserved for evaluation. Frames were
extracted at fixed intervals and de-duplicated with a perceptual hash, which also
guarantees that no training frame is a near-duplicate of an evaluation frame.
Annotation uses a single \texttt{person} class with segmentation-assisted boxes,
audited for degenerate and oversized boxes. The corpus holds 395 frames and
18{,}774 annotated people, 248 frames for training and 133 for evaluation, with
per-set composition in Table~\ref{tab:real}. Median subject size ranges from
17 px on disaster rubble to 44 px on drone footage, a wider range than C2A alone.

All configurations share one preprocessing pipeline, so no comparison reported
here is confounded by input handling. Images are letterbox-resized to
$640 \times 640$ with aspect ratio preserved, real footage after downscaling to
1{,}280 px on the long side. Training uses the framework default augmentation
with mosaic disabled for the final ten epochs. A stronger set stacking
copy-paste, mixup, vertical flipping and rotation was tested and rejected, since
it returned 0.3 points at the very-tiny band while costing 13.3 points of
medium-object recall.

\subsection{Attention and Scale}
The first change replaces the self-attention block that follows the
spatial-pyramid-pooling stage with a convolutional block attention
module~\cite{woo2018cbam}, which applies channel attention and then spatial
attention with a reduction ratio of 16 and a $7 \times 7$ spatial kernel. The
module is lighter than the block it replaces, so whatever it earns is earned at
a negative parameter cost.

The second change adds resolution. A detection cell covers a stride-sized
region, so a target of side $s$ spans $s/d$ cells at stride $d$: a twelve-pixel
person covers about 1.5 cells at stride 8, the finest standard level, and about
three at stride 4, which is the first level giving enough spatial support to
regress an offset from. The neck's top-down pass continues one level below the
standard design: the fused stride-8 feature is upsampled, concatenated with the
backbone feature at stride 4, and fused into a $160 \times 160$ map that a
fourth detection branch reads. The existing three levels are untouched, which
keeps the addition attributable. These two changes together are MSA-YOLO,
drawn in Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}.

\subsection{Tiny-Aware Label Assignment}
\label{sec:assign}
The third change leaves the network alone. Modern YOLO versions select positive
samples with a task-aligned assigner that scores each candidate by
$t = s^{p} u^{q}$, where $s$ is a classification score and $u$ a localization
overlap measured with the complete IoU. That measurement is the weak point at
this scale: for a person a few pixels wide a one-pixel offset can drive the IoU
to zero, so candidates for the smallest targets are ranked with almost no signal
and those people are trained on poorly chosen samples. The network can be made
as sharp as one likes and this will still be true.

The assigner is retained and only its overlap term is replaced. For a ground-truth box
$g$ and a candidate $b$ with centers, widths and heights $(c_x, c_y, w, h)$, the
squared 2-Wasserstein distance between Gaussians fitted to the boxes is
\begin{equation}
  W_2^2(g,b) = (c_{x}^{g}\!-\!c_{x}^{b})^2 + (c_{y}^{g}\!-\!c_{y}^{b})^2
    + \tfrac{1}{4}\big[(w^{g}\!-\!w^{b})^2 + (h^{g}\!-\!h^{b})^2\big],
\end{equation}
and the normalized Wasserstein similarity~\cite{wang2021nwd} maps it into
$(0,1]$ as $S(g,b) = \exp(-W_2(g,b)/C)$, where $C$ is a dataset-scale constant.
The value of 12.8 used by Wang et al.~\cite{wang2021nwd} is retained, since the
median person in C2A measures 12.0 px on the square root of area and so lies in
the same regime. Unlike the IoU, this similarity stays
informative for disjoint tiny boxes: a near miss scores above a distant one even
when both overlap nothing. The overlap the assigner consumes becomes
\begin{equation}
  u = (1-\gamma)\,\mathrm{CIoU}(g,b) + \gamma\, S(g,b),
  \label{eq:blend}
\end{equation}
where $\gamma$ sets how far assignment trusts the scale-tolerant similarity, and
$\gamma = 0$ recovers the unmodified assigner. The change affects training only.
It adds no parameters and no inference cost, and it is distinct from placing the
same distance inside the regression loss, which was also tested without success.

\subsection{A Rejected Alternative}
A fourth candidate, tested and not adopted, replaced the bottleneck at six neck
layers with a block performing bidirectional local-window selective scans in the
manner of Mamba~\cite{gu2023mamba} and its detection
adaptation~\cite{wang2025mambayolo}, leaving backbone and head unchanged. Scan diagnostics confirm it was active,
with a mean forward-to-reverse cosine distance of 0.836.

\section{Experiments and Results Analysis}

\subsection{Implementation Details}
All training and evaluation ran on one workstation with an NVIDIA RTX 4070 Ti
SUPER (16 GB), an Intel Core i7-14700K and 128 GB of RAM, using Python 3.11,
PyTorch 2.12 with CUDA 12.6, and Ultralytics 8.4.56. Every configuration used
AdamW at a base learning rate of 0.001 with a cosine schedule, up to 300 epochs
with early stopping on fitness and on $F_2$, mixed precision, and seed 0. The
optimizer was pinned because the framework default diverged on the four-scale
head. Batch size was 16, reduced to 8 physical with gradient accumulation for
the stride-4 models, whose extra scale raises activation memory.

\subsection{Datasets and Protocols}
C2A~\cite{nihal2024c2a} provides 10{,}215 images with roughly 360{,}000 person
instances across collapsed-building, fire, flood and traffic scenes. Two
protocols are used, and they are never mixed within a table. The \emph{official}
split (6{,}129 training, 2{,}043 validation, 2{,}043 test images) carries the
network comparison, because published results on this benchmark use it. An audit
of scene identifiers found that 98\% of official test scenes also appear in
training, since several composited images share one background. A
\emph{scene-disjoint} re-split (6{,}135 / 2{,}040 / 2{,}040) was therefore
constructed, in which all images of a scene fall on one side. Retraining the recommended model there costs
1.6 points of mAP@0.5 and 1.2 points of sub-8-pixel recall, so the leakage inflates
the official protocol modestly and changes no conclusion. The assignment and
real-footage experiments use the clean split.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
Because a missed person costs more than a false alarm, the primary metric is
recall, reported with the recall-weighted $F_2$ score. Global and size-local
measures are separated throughout, since a model may improve on one while losing
the other. Global measures are mean average precision at IoU 0.5 (mAP@0.5), the
same averaged over IoU 0.5 to 0.95 (mAP@0.5:0.95), and average recall at 100
detections. Size-local measures are average precision on small objects and
recall within the bands very-tiny (under 8 px), tiny (8 to 16), small (16 to 32)
and medium (32 to 96), where size is the square root of box area. All average
precision figures follow the COCO protocol. Latency is end-to-end, including
preprocessing and non-maximum suppression. Paired comparisons use an image-level
bootstrap over 1{,}000 resamples, giving a 95\% interval and a two-sided
$p$-value.

\label{sec:where}

\subsection{Ablation: Attention, Scale and Sequence Modeling}
Table~\ref{tab:abl} introduces one network change at a time on the official C2A
test set, grouped into global accuracy, size-local accuracy and cost.
Aggregate average precision is saturated: all four configurations lie within
0.002 of one another, which is consistent with annotation noise dominating at
strict IoU thresholds for boxes of this size. The configurations separate on the
size-local measures. The stride-4 scale accounts for most of the improvement.
Added on top of attention, it raises mAP@0.5 from 0.847 to 0.853, average recall
from 0.692 to 0.703, sub-8-pixel recall from 0.746 to 0.757 and $F_2$ from 0.841

substitution is comparatively inexpensive, since it removes 0.96 million
parameters relative to the baseline, yields the lowest latency of the four
configurations and improves each accuracy measure marginally. The state-space
neck is the only configuration that does not repay its cost, adding 2.4 million
parameters and raising latency from 14.6 to 41.1 ms while matching rather than
exceeding the preceding configuration. MSA-YOLO is therefore defined as the

Recall by size explains the flat aggregate. The stride-4 scale trades a little
accuracy on larger targets for its gain at the bottom: tiny-band recall moves
from 0.873 to 0.865 and small-band recall from 0.895 to 0.886, while the medium
band falls from 0.912 to 0.808. That last figure looks severe but rests on 317
of roughly 72{,}500 test instances, so it is noisy. The net effect is a model
that is better where the dataset lives, since 99.6\% of instances are below
32 px. Calibration is stable across the chain, with expected calibration error
between 0.020 and 0.023. Two inference-time procedures were also measured
without retraining: slicing~\cite{akyon2022sahi} at a 256-pixel tile raises
sub-8-pixel recall to 0.788 at 162 ms per image against 15 ms, and test-time
augmentation at 1280 px reaches 0.850 at 60 ms. Both exchange latency for recall.

\subsection{Ablation: Label Assignment}
The assignment blend of Equation~\eqref{eq:blend} was evaluated on the
scene-disjoint split, so its numbers are not comparable to Table~\ref{tab:abl}
and are reported as a matched difference. Fifty-epoch pilots swept $\gamma$ and
found a monotonic trade: sub-8-pixel recall rises from 0.693 at $\gamma = 0$ to
0.712 at $\gamma = 0.5$ and 0.729 at $\gamma = 1$, while small-object average
precision falls from 0.560 to 0.553 and then to 0.505. The half blend takes about
half of the available recall gain for an eighth of the precision cost, and was
carried forward.

Table~\ref{tab:assign} reports the decisive test, a matched pair at the full
protocol in which two runs share launcher, initialization, split and schedule
and differ only in $\gamma$. The blend raises sub-8-pixel recall from 0.730 to
0.751, a gain of 2.08 points whose 95\% bootstrap interval $[1.77, 2.38]$
excludes zero at $p < 0.001$, and it holds the overall operating score while
paying about one point in each of the two bands above. Retraining both models at
two further seeds reproduces the effect: counting the pair above as the first,
the three seeds give 2.08, 1.66 and 1.11 points, a mean of 1.62 with a standard
deviation of 0.49, positive in every seed. One sub-claim did not
survive that replication: a two-point small-object average-precision gain seen at
seed 0 averaged to noise across seeds and is therefore not claimed.

The magnitude of this effect is the principal observation of this work. A
modification that adds no parameters, no floating-point operations and no
latency improves recall in the hardest size band by more than the three network
modifications achieve together, 2.08 points against 1.40. The two figures derive
from different protocols and therefore compare effect sizes rather than absolute
levels, but the ordering is unambiguous.

Adaptation is a supervised fine-tune requiring no mechanism beyond data mixing:
the full synthetic training split plus the 248 real frames oversampled twenty
times, at a reduced learning rate of $5 \times 10^{-4}$ for at most 80 epochs.

Zero-shot transfer is weak, and performance on the synthetic benchmark does not
predict it. The model attaining 0.844 mAP@0.5 on C2A reaches only 0.335 on the
drone test set and 0.133 on real disaster frames. Adaptation closes most of the drone gap and part
of the disaster gap, as Table~\ref{tab:multi} and Fig.~\ref{fig:qual} show. In
that figure a red box covering a green one is a person found and a lone green
box is a person missed, so the drone row shows adaptation covering most of the
ground truth while the disaster row leaves many green boxes uncovered.
The drone score reaches 0.795, and recall at the operating threshold of 0.25
rises from 0.337 to 0.837, recovering 2{,}998 of 3{,}584 people against 1{,}206
previously. Disaster footage from the training events reaches 0.411,
approximately three times its zero-shot value. Fine-tuning on drone footage alone leaves disaster
frames unchanged at 0.133 to 0.124, indicating that the improvement derives from
disaster annotations specifically rather than from exposure to real imagery in
general. Performance on the synthetic benchmark is largely preserved, declining
from 0.844 to 0.837 while calibration error improves from 0.014 to 0.011, so a
single model serves both domains.

Three boundaries qualify this result. First, the adaptation is specific to the
footage observed during training. On the held-out event the adapted model scores
0.413 against 0.396 for the base model, a difference of 1.7 points whose
bootstrap interval spans zero ($p = 0.47$), while precision at the operating
threshold falls by 13.1 points. Sixty frames drawn from two events therefore
adapt the model to those events rather than to disaster imagery in general.
Second, the absolute level on real disaster footage remains modest, since the
adapted model recovers 482 of 1{,}070 people at the operating threshold and
approximately half of its detections are false alarms, which suits
operator-reviewed triage rather than autonomous decision-making. Third, the size
regime this work targets is untouched on real data: no model tested here recovers
any of the 54 instances below 8 px in real disaster frames. Every ablation gain
reported above was measured on composited imagery and none of it extends to the
bottom of the size range on real imagery.

\section{Conclusion}
This work examined the sources of sub-8-pixel recall by evaluating four
candidate modifications on a single detector under one protocol. The attention
substitution contributes 0.3 points at a reduced parameter count, and a stride-4
detection scale a further 1.1 points, which together define MSA-YOLO at 0.853
mAP@0.5 and 19.6 million parameters, second among published C2A results to a
model three times its size. A bidirectional state-space neck provides no
improvement for 2.4 million parameters and 2.8 times the latency. The assignment
of supervision contributes 2.08 points at no inference cost, exceeding all three
network modifications and indicating that for targets of this size the
allocation of supervision is more consequential than architectural addition. On
real footage the same detector attains 0.335 mAP@0.5 on drone video and 0.133 on
disaster frames, which 248 annotated frames raise to 0.795 and 0.411 at minimal
cost to benchmark accuracy, while nothing below 8 px is recovered. These results
suggest that further progress in this regime depends more on supervision and on
real annotated data than on additional network capacity.
