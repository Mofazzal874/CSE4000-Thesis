\frontheading{Abstract}

Finding people in aerial disaster imagery is a search-and-rescue problem in
which the targets are often only a few pixels wide, partly occluded, and set
against rubble, water, or smoke. Detectors tuned for ordinary object sizes lose
them, and published work on the task reports strong aggregate scores without
isolating which design change earns them. This thesis presents a compact
real-time detector for that regime together with the evidence for each of its
parts: four configurations were trained under a single frozen protocol on the
C2A disaster-imagery benchmark, beginning from a YOLO11m baseline and adding,
one change at a time, a channel-and-spatial attention module, a stride-4
detection scale, and a bidirectional state-space neck. Because a benchmark
result can be an artefact of how the data is divided, those conclusions were
then retested on a scene-disjoint partition of the same images, under a
modified label-assignment rule, and on real footage from drone flights, campus
scenes, and disaster news video. Of the three architectural changes only the
stride-4 scale mattered, raising $\mathrm{AP}_{50}$ from $0.843$ to $0.853$;
the state-space neck cost a factor of $2.8$ in latency for no accuracy gain and
was rejected. The larger improvement on the smallest targets came not from
architecture but from supervision, as blending a scale-tolerant similarity into
label assignment raised recall below eight pixels by $1.6$ points across
replicated seeds while leaving the coarser size bands unchanged. That advantage
did not carry to real imagery unaided, where the synthetically trained model
reached $0.335$~$\mathrm{AP}_{50}$ until adaptation on a few hundred labelled
frames lifted it to $0.795$. The resulting detector uses $19.6$~million
parameters and runs in $14.6$~ms per image, and the wider lesson is that at
this target size the supervision signal is worth more than added network
capacity.

\section{Introduction}
When an earthquake, flood, or fire strikes, the first hours decide how many
people are found alive. Uncrewed aerial vehicles can survey a disaster site far
faster than ground teams, and they do so without risking a rescuer. The imagery
they return, however, is difficult to search by eye. A person photographed from
altitude may occupy fewer than ten pixels among rubble, water, or smoke, and a
single sortie can produce thousands of frames. An operator scanning that stream
will miss people. Automating the search is therefore a practical need, not a
convenience, and it is the problem this thesis addresses.

Object detection itself is a mature field. Two-stage detectors in the R-CNN
line~\cite{ren2015fasterrcnn} established high accuracy at high cost, one-stage
detectors in the YOLO family~\cite{redmon2016yolo, jocher2024yolo11} made
detection fast enough for live video, and transformer designs such as
RT-DETR~\cite{zhao2024rtdetr} removed hand-built components. None of this
maturity transfers cleanly to aerial search and rescue. The targets are an
order of magnitude smaller than the objects these detectors were tuned for,
and the published remedies for small objects, extra detection scales, feature
attention, and sliced inference~\cite{akyon2022sahi}, are usually evaluated one
at a time, on generic aerial benchmarks, and without a shared training
protocol. State-space (Mamba) modules~\cite{gu2023mamba} have recently been
promoted for detection as well, again without a controlled test on very small
human targets. The literature therefore gives limited guidance on which
addition is worth its cost in a deployable rescue detector.

This thesis replaces that guess with a measurement. Starting from the YOLO11m
baseline, it adds one component at a time, attention, a high-resolution
detection scale, and a state-space neck, trains every configuration under one
frozen protocol on the C2A disaster-imagery benchmark~\cite{nihal2024c2a}, and
reports what each change buys in accuracy, parameters, and latency. The outcome
is a compact detector intended for resource-constrained deployment, together with an
evidence trail that justifies every design decision in it. A second phase of
the work then pressure-tests that outcome: it audits the benchmark split for
scene leakage, moves the search for further gains from the architecture into
the label-assignment step of training, and confronts the model with real
aerial footage collected and annotated for this thesis, including a disaster
event held out from all training.

\section{Problem Statement}
Given an aerial image $I$, a detector must output a set of bounding boxes
$\hat{B} = \{(x, y, w, h, c)\}$, one per visible person, each with a confidence
score $c$. A prediction is counted as correct when it overlaps a ground-truth
box $B$ sufficiently, measured by intersection over union,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{IoU}(\hat{B}, B) =
    \frac{\mathrm{area}(\hat{B} \cap B)}{\mathrm{area}(\hat{B} \cup B)},
  \label{eq:iou}
\end{equation}
with $\mathrm{IoU} \geq 0.5$ used as the matching threshold throughout this
work. Stated this way the task sounds ordinary. What makes it hard is the
geometry of aerial imagery.

A one-stage detector predicts boxes on a grid whose cell size equals the
feature stride $d$. A target of side $s$ pixels spans
\begin{equation}
  c_d(s) = \frac{s}{d}
  \label{eq:coverage}
\end{equation}
cells in each direction. The finest standard YOLO scale detects at stride
$d = 8$, so a 12-px person spans about 1.5 cells there, and only 0.4 of a cell
at the stride-32 scale. A target represented by only a fraction of one cell
provides little spatial support for localisation. In the C2A benchmark used here, about 47\% of the
annotated people are smaller than ten pixels~\cite{nihal2024c2a}, which places
nearly half the dataset at or below the resolving limit of a standard
detection head. Scenes carry 20 to 40 people each, many partially buried or
occluded, against backgrounds of rubble, flood water, fire, and wrecked
vehicles. Figure~\ref{fig:introscene} shows the kind of scene an operator
faces.

\begin{figure}[tbp]
  \centering
  \figorplaceholder{figures/fig_intro_scene.png}{6.5cm}
  \caption{Aerial drone footage of a flood-affected region~\cite{agiindia2024drones}.}
  \label{fig:introscene}
\end{figure}

One more property of the domain shapes the whole thesis. In search and rescue,
a missed person may not get a second chance, while a false alarm costs an
operator a few seconds of review. Recall therefore matters more than
precision, and the operational metric used throughout is the recall-weighted
$F_2$ score,
\begin{equation}
  F_2 = \frac{5PR}{4P + R},
  \label{eq:f2}
\end{equation}
where $P$ is precision and $R$ is recall. The engineering question of this
thesis is then concrete: which changes to a compact one-stage detector raise
recall on sub-ten-pixel people without an unacceptable increase in parameters
or desktop-GPU latency?

\section{Objectives}

The problem described above sets a clear target: a detector that finds people
only a few pixels across, in cluttered disaster scenes, at a speed and size that
a drone-borne computer can carry. That target breaks into four objectives, which
move from building the model, to measuring what each part of it contributes, to
testing whether the result survives outside the benchmark it was tuned on. They
are stated below and revisited in the conclusion against what was achieved.

\begin{itemize}
  \item To build a compact detector for very small human targets in aerial
        disaster imagery by refining the YOLO11m baseline through an additive
        ablation, in which channel-and-spatial attention (CBAM), a stride-4
        (P2) detection scale, and a state-space neck are introduced one at a
        time and measured under an identical training and evaluation protocol.
  \item To identify, from those measurements, the configuration that best
        serves very-small-target recall within a deployable parameter and
        latency budget, and to show it performs close to the published state
        of the art on the same benchmark.
  \item To test the recent claim that state-space modules help detection, on
        this task and under this protocol, and to report the outcome whether
        or not it favours the module.
  \item To characterise the recall--latency trade-off of slicing-aided
        inference and test-time augmentation without changing the trained
        weights.
\end{itemize}

\section{Scope}

Stating what the study does not cover matters as much as stating what it does,
because several of the limits shape how far the results can be read. The
boundaries fall under four headings: the data the detector was trained and tested
on, the form of its input, the parts of the architecture that were allowed to
change, and the protocol under which every number was measured. Each is fixed
before the experiments begin and held constant throughout, so that differences
between configurations can be attributed to the configuration and nothing else.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Dataset.} The primary benchmark is C2A~\cite{nihal2024c2a}
        with its frozen split of 6{,}129 training, 2{,}043 validation, and
        2{,}043 test images, and a single object class, \texttt{person}; the
        extended phase adds a scene-disjoint re-split of the same data and
        real footage collected for this thesis (drone flights at three
        altitudes, campus scenes, and frames from news video of three
        disaster events). Cross-dataset generalisation to public collections
        such as SARD remains future work.
  \item \textbf{Input.} The detector operates on single RGB frames,
        letterbox-resized to $640 \times 640$ for training. Video tracking,
        thermal imagery, and multi-sensor fusion are outside the scope.
  \item \textbf{Architecture.} The study covers attention substitution
        (CBAM), an added stride-4 detection scale, and a state-space neck as
        architectural changes, SAHI slicing with test-time augmentation as
        inference-time settings, and, in the extended phase, a Wasserstein
        blend in the label assigner and a supervised fine-tune as
        training-time changes. It does not redesign the backbone or search
        architectures automatically.
  \item \textbf{Evaluation.} All results come from the COCO protocol plus an
        operational evaluation at $\mathrm{IoU} = 0.5$, run on a single
        consumer GPU (RTX 4070 Ti SUPER). No field trial with a physical
        drone is included.
\end{itemize}

\section{Unfamiliarity of the Problem}
No prior solution was reused wholesale. The individual ingredients are
published, and are cited where they appear, but the specific design and the
questions asked of it are this work's own. The additive, single-variable
ablation chain on disaster imagery does not appear in prior C2A work, which
reports whole models against whole models~\cite{nihal2024c2a} and leaves open
where a gain actually comes from. The state-space experiment places
bidirectional local-window Mamba blocks inside the \emph{neck} of an
otherwise unchanged CNN detector, whereas published Mamba detectors replace
the backbone~\cite{wang2025mambayolo}, which prevents a like-for-like
attribution. The attention change is a substitution rather than a bolt-on: it
replaces the baseline C2PSA module, reduces the parameter count, and preserves
the measured accuracy. A negative result is treated as a first-class finding,
documented with
the same rigour as the successful components, because knowing what not to
deploy is part of the engineering answer.

\section{Contribution}

What this thesis offers is less a single new module than a controlled account of
which changes to a compact detector actually help on sub-ten-pixel targets, and
which do not. The value lies in the protocol as much as the result: every
configuration is trained and evaluated identically, so each reported gain can be
attributed to one architectural change rather than to a difference in schedule,
augmentation, or split. Negative outcomes are reported on the same footing as
positive ones. Nine contributions follow from that discipline, six from the
additive ablation and three from the extended phase that builds on it.

\begin{itemize}
  \item A four-configuration additive ablation on C2A, trained and evaluated
        under one frozen protocol, that attributes every gain to a single
        architectural change.
  \item A CBAM substitution that replaces the baseline's C2PSA module and
        removes roughly one million parameters while preserving the
        operational scores, with the lowest latency of the four configurations.
  \item A stride-4 (P2) detection scale whose effect is quantified rather
        than assumed: $\mathrm{AP}_{50}$ rises from 0.843 to 0.853 and
        very-tiny recall from 0.743 to 0.757, at about half a million
        parameters and one millisecond of latency.
  \item A controlled negative result for the state-space neck: 2.4 million
        added parameters and 2.8 times the latency for no accuracy change,
        which argues against adopting such modules for this task on current
        evidence.
  \item An exploratory SAHI/TTA study on an older checkpoint, retained as a
        latency--recall comparison but kept separate from the recommended model.
  \item A recommended detector of 19.6 million parameters that matches or
        exceeds every published C2A baseline except YOLOv9-e while using
        about a third of that model's parameters.
  \item From the extended phase: a scene-leakage audit of the benchmark with
        a scene-disjoint re-split, quantifying the leakage at 1.6 points of
        $\mathrm{AP}_{50}$.
  \item A tiny-aware label-assignment blend inside the task-aligned assigner
        that raises sub-8-pixel recall in all three training seeds tested
        (mean 1.6 points; 2.1 in the matched pair, with a bootstrap
        confidence interval that excludes zero), at no parameter or latency
        cost, together with a second documented architecture negative.
  \item An annotated real-imagery test suite (own drone footage at three
        altitudes, campus scenes, and disaster news frames) and a supervised
        adaptation study on it, showing a few hundred labelled frames close
        most of the drone-domain gap at almost no cost to the source
        benchmark, while a held-out disaster event bounds how far that
        adaptation generalises.
\end{itemize}

\section{Application of the Work}

Search and rescue is the setting this work was built for, but the underlying
problem, finding small human figures in imagery taken from height, recurs
wherever a camera looks down on people from a distance. The five applications
below are ordered from the closest to that original setting to the furthest from
it. The last is less an application than a property of the design: a model of
this size can run where a larger one cannot, which is what makes the others
practical in the field rather than only in a laboratory.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Search and rescue triage.} The immediate application: a
        drone or a laptop at an incident site flags likely victims in live
        aerial imagery so operators review candidates instead of raw frames.
  \item \textbf{Disaster damage assessment.} The same detections, aggregated
        over a survey flight, tell relief planners where people are
        concentrated after a flood or earthquake.
  \item \textbf{Crowd monitoring.} Counting very small people from altitude
        is the same technical problem, so the model transfers to public-event
        safety monitoring.
  \item \textbf{Wide-area security.} Border and coastline patrols scan large
        areas for isolated people, a low-density variant of the same task.
  \item \textbf{Embedded deployment.} At 19.6 million parameters the design
        fits consumer and edge GPUs, and the same recipe transfers to a
        smaller YOLO11 backbone for fully on-board operation.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Introduction}
The detector design draws on four lines of work: real-time object detection,
small-object feature pyramids, tiny-object label assignment, and aerial search
and rescue. They are reviewed in that order, followed by the specific components
modified in this thesis: YOLO11, CBAM and ECA, multi-scale detection, and visual
state-space models. The final section identifies which comparisons remain absent
from the C2A literature and turns them into the experimental questions of
Chapter~III.

\DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{Five strands of published work bear on this problem. The first traces how
detection architectures arrived at the real-time single-stage designs used here,
and the next two cover the two families of remedy proposed for small targets,

\DIFaddend \subsection{From Two-Stage Detectors to Real-Time and Transformer Models}
Modern object detection divides into two-stage and one-stage families. Two-stage
detectors, from R-CNN~\cite{girshick2014rcnn} to Faster R-CNN~\cite{ren2015fasterrcnn}
and its Cascade variant~\cite{cai2018cascade}, first propose regions and then
classify them, which gives strong accuracy at a high computational cost. One-stage
detectors such as SSD~\cite{liu2016ssd} predict boxes directly and trade a little
accuracy for speed; RetinaNet~\cite{lin2017focal} addressed the resulting
class-imbalance with the focal loss, and the YOLO family, from the original
design~\cite{redmon2016yolo} through successive versions
\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{\mbox{%DIFAUXCMD
\cite{redmon2018yolov3,bochkovskiy2020yolov4,wang2023yolov7,
jocher2023yolov8,wang2024yolov9,wang2024yolov10}
}\hskip0pt%DIFAUXCMD
}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{\mbox{%DIFAUXCMD
\cite{wang2023yolov7,
jocher2023yolov8,wang2024yolov9,wang2024yolov10}
}\hskip0pt%DIFAUXCMD
}\DIFaddend to YOLO11~\cite{jocher2024yolo11}, made single-pass detection the practical standard
for real-time use. A third, transformer-based family reduces reliance on
hand-designed components: DETR~\cite{carion2020detr} posed detection as set
prediction and removed anchors and non-maximum suppression from its inference
pipeline, while RT-DETR~\cite{zhao2024rtdetr} later made this approach
competitive with YOLO at real-time speeds. This thesis works within the one-stage
YOLO family because its speed and small model size suit airborne deployment.

\subsection{Small-Object and Aerial Detection}
Detecting very small objects is a distinct problem, surveyed in detail by Cheng et
al.~\cite{cheng2023smallobject}, because targets occupy few pixels and are lost in
the coarse strides of a standard detection head; drone benchmarks such as
VisDrone~\cite{zhu2021visdrone} make this the dominant difficulty. Three lines of
work address it. The first adds higher-resolution detection scales: feature
pyramids~\cite{lin2017fpn} and path-aggregation necks fuse fine and coarse features,
and adding a stride-4 (P2) head extends detection to targets a few pixels wide;
TPH-YOLOv5~\cite{zhu2021tphyolov5} combined such a head with transformer prediction
for drone imagery. The second re-weights features with attention so that faint
targets are not suppressed by background clutter; channel attention
(ECA~\cite{wang2020eca}) and combined channel-and-spatial attention
(CBAM~\cite{woo2018cbam}) are the common choices. The third operates at inference
time: slicing-aided hyper inference (SAHI~\cite{akyon2022sahi}) runs the detector
over overlapping crops so that small targets are effectively enlarged. All three are
relevant here; the thesis studies the first two as architectural changes and the
third as an inference-time setting.

\subsection{Label Assignment for Tiny Objects}
A fourth line of work changes neither the network nor the input but the
training step that decides which predictions count as positive samples. Its
starting observation is that IoU-based matching breaks down for tiny boxes: a
one-pixel shift between a candidate and a ground-truth box a few pixels wide
can drive their IoU to zero, so the assigner ranks candidates for the smallest
targets almost arbitrarily. Xu et al.~\cite{xu2022nwdrka} replaced the IoU
with a normalised Wasserstein distance between Gaussians fitted to the boxes,
which decays smoothly with offset instead of collapsing, and reported the
largest gains when the substitution is made in the assignment step rather than
in the regression loss. RFLA~\cite{xu2022rfla} built a full assignment strategy
on a receptive-field Gaussian prior, and SimD~\cite{shi2024simd} learned an
adaptive location-and-shape similarity for the same purpose. On the benchmarks
reported by these studies, assignment-side changes improve tiny-object accuracy
more consistently than loss-only variants. Modern YOLO
versions select their training samples with the task-aligned assigner of
TOOD~\cite{feng2021tood}, which still scores localisation with the IoU family,
and none of the tiny-object assignment work above has been applied inside it
for aerial person detection. That opening is taken up in Chapter~III.

\subsection{Human Detection in Disaster and Search-and-Rescue Imagery}
Aerial human detection for search and rescue has been surveyed as a benchmark
problem in its own right~\cite{sar_survey}, and prior work has applied deep models
to aerial video for this purpose~\cite{aerial_human_video}. A recurring obstacle is
data: real annotated disaster imagery with very small human targets is scarce. Real
search-and-rescue datasets such as SARD~\cite{sambolek2021sard} and
HERIDAL~\cite{bozic2019heridal} are valuable but limited in scale and scene variety.
Nihal et al.~\cite{nihal2024c2a} addressed this with C2A, a semi-synthetic dataset
that composites human poses, segmented with a U$^2$-Net model~\cite{qin2020u2net},
onto disaster backgrounds drawn from aerial emergency
imagery~\cite{kyrkou2020aider}, and benchmarked a range of detectors on it. Their
study is the direct point of comparison for this thesis, which uses the same dataset
and test split.

Training on composited data raises the question of transfer to real imagery.
Work on domain randomisation showed that models trained on varied synthetic
scenes can transfer to the real world~\cite{tobin2017domainrand}, and that
fine-tuning a synthetic-trained model on a small amount of real data beats
training on the real data alone~\cite{tremblay2018synthetic}. The practical
recipe that follows, and the one examined in the extended phase of this
thesis, is to train on the large synthetic set, adapt with whatever real
labels can be afforded, and then measure what remains of the gap.

\subsection{State-Space Models for Vision}
State-space models have recently been proposed as a linear-time alternative to
attention for long-sequence modelling. Building on structured state
spaces~\cite{gu2022s4}, Mamba~\cite{gu2023mamba} introduced a selective scan that
makes the state dynamics input-dependent. Vision adaptations followed: Vision
Mamba~\cite{zhu2024visionmamba} and VMamba~\cite{liu2024vmamba} scan image patches in
multiple directions, and Mamba-YOLO~\cite{wang2025mambayolo} applied the idea to
detection by replacing the backbone with state-space blocks. The variant explored in
this thesis is narrower: it keeps
the convolutional backbone and inserts bidirectional local-window state-space
blocks only in the neck, which allows its effect to be measured against an
otherwise identical model.

\section{Core Concepts}
The following components form the baseline or one of its tested variants. Their
modifications are specified in Chapter~III.

\subsection{The YOLO11 Baseline}
YOLO11~\cite{jocher2024yolo11} is a single-stage detector with three parts: a
convolutional backbone that extracts features at decreasing spatial resolution
through C3k2 stages, a path-aggregation neck (PAN--FPN) that fuses features across
scales, and a decoupled detection head that predicts boxes at three strides (P3,
P4, P5). The backbone ends with a spatial-pyramid-pooling (SPPF) stage and a
C2PSA self-attention block. Its medium variant, YOLO11m, is the baseline used
throughout this thesis; it was chosen from a nine-model comparison of the
YOLOv9, YOLOv10, and YOLO11 families reported in the appendix
(Table~\ref{tab:appfamily} and Figure~\ref{fig:appfrontier}), where it sits on
the best accuracy-to-size trade-off.

\subsection{Attention: CBAM and ECA}
Attention modules re-weight feature maps so that informative channels and spatial
locations are emphasised. Squeeze-and-Excitation networks~\cite{hu2018senet} first
popularised channel re-weighting; ECA~\cite{wang2020eca} makes it lightweight with a
one-dimensional convolution. CBAM~\cite{woo2018cbam} is stronger:
it applies channel attention followed by spatial attention in sequence, as shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:cbam}. An earlier study on this dataset compared both against the
baseline and found CBAM to give better operational recall than ECA at a similar
cost, which is why CBAM is used in this thesis; that comparison is summarised in
Chapter~IV.

\subsection{Multi-Scale Detection and the P2 Scale}
The finest standard detection scale, P3 at stride~8, summarises an $8\times8$ pixel
region per grid cell at $640$ input, which is coarse for targets only a few pixels
across. Adding a P2 scale at stride~4 introduces a $160\times160$ feature map whose
cells cover $4\times4$ pixels, extending detection to targets as small as about
four pixels. Figure~\ref{fig:p2} shows how the P2 branch attaches alongside the
standard P3--P5 scales; it provides a finer prediction grid but does not, by

whose dynamics, in the selective (Mamba) formulation, depend on the input. Applied
to vision, a feature map is scanned as a sequence. The block explored in this
thesis, C3k2Mamba (Figure~\ref{fig:mamba}), scans a local window in both the forward
and reverse directions and fuses the two, and is inserted in place of selected C3k2

\DIFaddend \subsection{Research Gap and Proposed Solution}
The reviewed work leaves a specific gap. Small-object techniques, namely extra
detection scales, attention, and inference-time slicing, are usually reported in

\section{Introduction}
The methodology treats very small aerial-human detection as a
resolution-and-attention problem: the targets of
interest are frequently smaller than the finest detection cell of a standard
one-stage detector, and they sit in heavy background clutter. The proposed model
therefore modifies the YOLO11m baseline (reviewed in Chapter~II) in exactly two
places: a Convolutional Block Attention Module (CBAM) replaces the C2PSA attention
block at the end of the backbone, and the neck and head are extended with a
stride-4 (P2) detection scale. A third component, a bidirectional state-space
(Mamba) neck block, was explored under the same protocol and produced a negative
result. Rather than adopting a single fixed architecture, the model
is developed through an \emph{additive ablation}: one architectural change is
introduced at a time so that the contribution of each component can be attributed
independently. The layer-level specification is followed by the loss,
label-assignment, real-data adaptation, and inference procedures evaluated in
Chapter~IV.

\section{System Architecture Overview}
The complete pipeline is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:framework}. Given an aerial
image, the system letterbox-resizes it to $640 \times 640$, extracts multi-scale
features with the YOLO11m backbone, refines the deepest features with CBAM,
fuses all scales in a PAN--FPN neck extended down to stride~4, and decodes boxes
at four scales before non-maximum suppression (NMS) produces the final human
detections.

The workflow has three phases:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Feature extraction and attention.} The backbone produces feature
  maps at strides 2--32 (P1--P5). At the deepest level, CBAM applies channel
  attention followed by spatial attention to the 512-channel P5 feature map
  before fusion. This is a learned re-weighting operation; it is not assumed to
  isolate people by itself.
  \item \textbf{Multi-scale fusion with a P2 extension.} The neck runs the usual
  top-down and bottom-up passes, but the top-down pass continues one level
  further than the standard design: it upsamples to stride~4 and fuses the
  high-resolution backbone P2 features, producing a $160 \times 160$ map whose
  cells cover only $4 \times 4$ input pixels.
  \item \textbf{Four-scale decoding.} The head predicts boxes at strides 4, 8,
  16, and 32. NMS then suppresses duplicate boxes. In crowded regions this
  suppression can also remove a neighbouring person's prediction, which is
  treated as a residual failure mode rather than a benefit of NMS.
\end{itemize}

\section{Proposed System Architecture}
Figure~\ref{fig:fullarch} traces the complete data flow of the proposed
detector on a real C2A scene. An input image enters at the top left and passes
through the YOLO11m backbone, which produces feature maps at five decreasing
resolutions from P1 to P5. At the deepest level the CBAM block, drawn in
orange, replaces the original C2PSA attention and re-weights the P5 features
before any fusion happens. The PAN--FPN neck then fuses the scales in a
top-down and a bottom-up pass, and the fusion is carried one level further than
the standard design so that a high-resolution stride-4 (P2) branch is produced.
The four fused scales feed a single detection head, and non-maximum suppression
returns one box per recovered person. The image insets placed around the
  diagram are the model's own signals on this scene: the input, CBAM attention
  map, P2 feature response, and final detections. The CBAM map is spatially
  diffuse rather than a person-localisation map; the P2 response provides the
  finer spatial evidence used by the additional detection scale.

\subsection{Data Preprocessing}
All images are letterbox-resized to $640 \times 640$ so that the original aspect
ratio is preserved and no target is geometrically distorted, which matters when
the objects of interest are only a few pixels wide. Pixel values are normalised to
$[0,1]$, and only the default YOLO11 training-time augmentations are used
(mosaic, scaling, HSV jitter), with mosaic disabled for the final ten epochs
(\texttt{close\_mosaic}). Aggressive small-object augmentation (copy--paste,
mixup, vertical flip) was tested and discarded as a negative result, as reported
in Chapter~IV.

\subsection{Attention Enhancement: CBAM}
\label{sec:cbam3}
The baseline YOLO11m places one C2PSA module after the SPPF stage. In the
proposed model it is replaced by a single CBAM module, which applies
channel attention followed by spatial attention in sequence. Given an
intermediate feature map $\mathbf{F} \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times H \times W}$, CBAM
computes a channel attention map $\mathbf{M}_c$ and a spatial attention map
$\mathbf{M}_s$,
\begin{align}
  \mathbf{M}_c(\mathbf{F}) &= \sigma\!\big(\mathrm{MLP}(\mathrm{AvgPool}(\mathbf{F}))
      + \mathrm{MLP}(\mathrm{MaxPool}(\mathbf{F}))\big), \\
  \mathbf{M}_s(\mathbf{F}') &= \sigma\!\big(f^{7\times7}\big([\mathrm{AvgPool}(\mathbf{F}');
      \mathrm{MaxPool}(\mathbf{F}')]\big)\big),
\end{align}
where $\sigma$ is the sigmoid function and $f^{7\times7}$ a convolution with a
$7\times7$ kernel. The features are refined multiplicatively,
$\mathbf{F}' = \mathbf{M}_c(\mathbf{F}) \otimes \mathbf{F}$ and
$\mathbf{F}'' = \mathbf{M}_s(\mathbf{F}') \otimes \mathbf{F}'$, where $\otimes$
denotes element-wise multiplication with broadcasting. The module uses a channel
reduction ratio of 16 and a $7\times7$ spatial kernel, matching the original
design of Woo et al. Because CBAM is lighter than the C2PSA module it
replaces, this substitution \emph{reduces} the parameter count relative
to the baseline while adding both channel and spatial selectivity.

\subsection{Multi-Scale Detection: the P2 Head}
The standard YOLO11 head detects at three scales: P3, P4 and P5 (strides 8, 16
and 32). For $640 \times 640$ input, the finest of these (P3) operates on an
$80 \times 80$ grid, so each cell already summarises an $8 \times 8$ pixel
region; targets smaller than this are difficult to localise. The proposed model
adds a fourth detection level, P2, at stride~4. The construction follows the
neck's top-down pass one level further: the fused P3 neck feature
($80^2 \times 256$) is upsampled to $160^2$, concatenated with the
high-resolution backbone P2 feature ($160^2 \times 256$, the skip from
layer~2), and fused by a C3k2 block into the $160^2 \times 128$ P2 feature that
a stride-4 detection branch reads. Each P2 cell covers only a $4 \times 4$ pixel
region, giving a four-pixel target more candidate locations than the stride-8
grid. As shown in Chapter~IV, this high-resolution scale is the principal
driver of the improvement in very-small-target recall.

\subsection{State-Space Neck Variant: C3k2Mamba}
\label{sec:mamba}
To test whether a selective state-space model (SSM) can improve feature
aggregation in the neck, an explored variant replaces the C3k2 bottleneck at six
neck layers (indices 13, 16, 19, 22, 25 and 28) with a C3k2Mamba block. Each such
block performs bidirectional local-window scanning, a forward and a reverse
selective scan over a local window (window sizes 6--8, state dimension
$d_{\text{state}} = 4$), and is inserted by post-initialisation surgical
replacement so that the surrounding CNN backbone and the P2 head are unchanged.
This variant is included for completeness and evaluated under the same protocol;
Chapter~IV reports that it does not improve accuracy while increasing both the
parameter count and the inference latency, and it is therefore excluded from the
recommended model.

\section{Architecture Novelties}
Each departure from the baseline addresses a specific failure mode of
small-target detection, and each is
verified in isolation by the ablation of Chapter~IV. Three design decisions
define the contribution.

\subsection{Attention Substitution at Reduced Parameter Cost}
Aerial disaster scenes are dominated by distractors: rubble textures, water
glare, and vegetation produce strong generic feature responses that drown out
faint human signatures. The first design decision replaces the baseline C2PSA
module with one CBAM module. Figure~\ref{fig:cbameffect} illustrates the intended
channel-and-spatial re-weighting and includes a measured attention map. That map
is diffuse, so it does not establish that CBAM localises individual people. The
defensible result is narrower: the substitution removes about one million
parameters, preserves the operational scores, and gives the lowest latency
among the four evaluated configurations.

\subsection{A Detection Scale Matched to Target Size}
\label{sec:p2novel}
The second decision follows from the geometry in Figure~\ref{fig:strideprob}. A
detection cell summarises a stride-sized pixel region, so a $\sim$12-px human
occupies less than half of one P5 cell and under one P4 cell; even at P3 it spans
only about 1.5 cells. The added stride-4 scale is the first level at which such a
target covers about three cells, enough spatial support to be localised
precisely. On the C2A test split, $99.6\%$ of the human instances are smaller
than 32~px (Figure~\ref{fig:sizedist}), so the added level addresses the
dominant size range rather than a small subset of the dataset. The
branch is additive, and the existing P3--P5 pathway is left untouched, which is
what allows the ablation to attribute the very-tiny-recall gain to this single
change.

\subsection{Single-Variable Additive Design}
The third decision is methodological. The four configurations evaluated in this
thesis are summarised in Table~\ref{tab:variants}: each row introduces exactly
one change with respect to the preceding design, so that the effect of attention
(CBAM), of the high-resolution detection scale (P2), of their combination, and
of the state-space neck can each be isolated. All configurations are trained on
the same frozen C2A split with identical optimisation settings (AdamW,
$\text{lr}_0 = 0.001$, cosine schedule, up to 300 epochs with early stopping)
and evaluated with the same protocol and thresholds, so that every reported
difference is attributable to the architectural change alone. This design is
what makes the negative state-space result interpretable: the variant fails
under conditions in which the P2 head demonstrably succeeds.

\section{Loss Function Design}
\label{sec:loss3}
Training uses the standard YOLO11 composite detection loss, unchanged across all
four configurations so that the ablation compares architectures rather than
objectives. Predictions are matched to ground truth by the task-aligned
assigner, which scores each candidate cell by a product of its classification
confidence and its localisation quality, so that the cells that are best able to
both recognise and localise a target receive its supervision. Three terms are
then optimised.

Classification uses binary cross-entropy on the single \emph{person} class,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cls}} = -\big[\, y \log \hat{p} + (1-y)\log(1-\hat{p}) \,\big],
\end{equation}
where $\hat{p}$ is the predicted class probability and $y$ the
assigned label. Box regression uses the complete-IoU loss,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CIoU}} = 1 - \mathrm{IoU}
      + \frac{\rho^2(\mathbf{b}, \mathbf{b}^{gt})}{c^2} + \alpha v,
  \qquad
  v = \frac{4}{\pi^2}\Big(\arctan\tfrac{w^{gt}}{h^{gt}} - \arctan\tfrac{w}{h}\Big)^{\!2},
\end{equation}
where $\rho$ is the centre distance, $c$ the diagonal of the smallest enclosing
box, and $\alpha = v / \big((1-\mathrm{IoU}) + v\big)$ balances the aspect-ratio
term. The overlap, centre, and shape terms together matter for very small boxes,
whose IoU alone is unstable under sub-pixel shifts. Finally, box edges are
predicted as discrete distributions over binned offsets, trained with the
distribution focal loss,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DFL}} = -\big[(y_{i+1}-y)\log S_i + (y-y_i)\log S_{i+1}\big],
\end{equation}
which sharpens the predicted distribution around the continuous target offset
$y$ that falls between bins $y_i$ and $y_{i+1}$. The total objective is the
weighted sum
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L} = \lambda_{\mathrm{box}} \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CIoU}}
              + \lambda_{\mathrm{dfl}} \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DFL}}
              + \lambda_{\mathrm{cls}} \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cls}},
\end{equation}
with the framework defaults $\lambda_{\mathrm{box}} = 7.5$,
$\lambda_{\mathrm{dfl}} = 1.5$, and $\lambda_{\mathrm{cls}} = 0.5$. The heavy
box weighting suits this task: with a single class, the difficulty is not
deciding \emph{what} an object is but \emph{where} a few-pixel target sits. The
added P2 level contributes its own predictions to all three terms exactly as
the standard levels do, so no loss re-balancing is needed when the scale is
added.

\section{Extended Training Methods}
The methods so far define the architecture and its loss. A second phase of the
work, reported in Section~\ref{sec:partb}, left both untouched and changed how
the detector is trained instead. Three methods belong to that phase: a
modification of the label-assignment step for the smallest targets, an
architecture branch that was built and rejected, and a supervised procedure
that adapts the trained model to real footage.

\subsection{Tiny-Aware Label Assignment}
\label{sec:assignmethod}
Section~\ref{sec:loss3} noted that the task-aligned assigner scores each
candidate by the product of its classification confidence and its localisation
quality, and that localisation quality is measured with the IoU family. That
measurement is the weak point at this scale. For a person a few pixels wide, a
one-pixel offset between candidate and ground truth can drive the IoU to zero,
so the ranking among candidates for the smallest targets carries almost no
signal and the smallest people are trained on poorly chosen positive samples.
Work on tiny-object benchmarks reached the same diagnosis and moved the fix
into assignment rather than the loss, as reviewed in Chapter~II.

The modification keeps the assigner and replaces only its overlap term. For a
ground-truth box $g$ and a candidate $b$ with centres $(c_x, c_y)$, widths $w$,
and heights $h$, the squared 2-Wasserstein distance between Gaussians fitted to
the two boxes is
\begin{equation}
  W_2^2(g,b) = (c_{x}^{g}-c_{x}^{b})^2 + (c_{y}^{g}-c_{y}^{b})^2
             + \tfrac{1}{4}\big[(w^{g}-w^{b})^2 + (h^{g}-h^{b})^2\big],
\end{equation}
and the normalised Wasserstein similarity of Xu et al.~\cite{xu2022nwdrka}
maps it into $(0,1]$ as
\begin{equation}
  S(g,b) = \exp\!\big(-\,W_2(g,b)\,/\,C\big),
\end{equation}
with the constant $C=12.8$ set to the median person size of the dataset in
pixels. Unlike the IoU, this similarity stays informative for disjoint tiny
boxes: a near-miss candidate scores above a distant one even when both overlap
nothing. The overlap term the assigner uses becomes the blend
\begin{equation}
  u = (1-\gamma)\,\mathrm{CIoU}(g,b) + \gamma\, S(g,b),
  \label{eq:blend}
\end{equation}
where $\gamma \in [0,1]$ sets how far the assignment trusts the Wasserstein
similarity over the IoU, and $\gamma=0$ recovers the unmodified assigner.
Figure~\ref{fig:assignmech} places the blend inside the assignment pipeline.
The change affects training only. It adds no parameters and no inference cost,
and it is distinct from putting the Wasserstein distance inside the regression
loss, which was also tried and did not help. Chapter~IV reports the sweep over
$\gamma$ and a matched comparison at $\gamma=0.5$.

\subsection{A Frequency-Gated Evidence Branch}
\label{sec:fccgmethod}
The assignment change was adopted only after a second architecture branch
failed, and the branch is described here so that its negative result in
Chapter~IV is interpretable. A degradation probe had shown that near-lossless
JPEG re-compression of the test images collapses detection accuracy, which
means the tiny-object signal lives almost entirely in the highest
spatial-frequency band. The branch made that band explicit: it extracts
high-frequency evidence from the finest feature maps and multiplies it by a
gate computed with large-kernel convolutions from the surrounding context, the
intent being to keep high-frequency responses where context suggests a person
and to suppress them over clutter. The gated evidence feeds back into the two
finest fusion points of the neck at a cost of $0.55$~M parameters. The gates
train stably and stay active, so the null result is not explained by an
inactive or disconnected implementation. The experiment does not establish
whether the branch is redundant with CBAM and P2 or whether its evidence is
simply unhelpful for the detector.

\subsection{Supervised Adaptation to Real Imagery}
\label{sec:adaptmethod}
The third method carries the trained detector from composited training data to
real footage. Real frames came from three sources: the author's own drone
flights over open ground at 10, 30, and 50~m altitude, recorded in 4K; campus
scenes with partial occlusion from the same platform; and frames sampled from
published news footage of two disaster events, a flood and a building
collapse. Frames were extracted at fixed intervals, de-duplicated with a
perceptual hash, and annotated by the author with segmentation-assisted box
drawing, boxing every visible person and keeping frames with no people as
explicit negatives. An automated audit for degenerate, duplicate, undersized,
and oversized boxes, plus a visual overlay check of every frame, preceded any
training use. Evaluation frames are frozen, and the same perceptual hash
guarantees that no training frame is a near-duplicate of any evaluation frame.

The three flight altitudes deserve justification, because they are lower than
the altitude at which search-and-rescue survey is normally flown. Operational
guidance places routine SAR survey near 100~m above ground level, where the
trade between area coverage and image detail is balanced. What governs detector
difficulty, however, is not the altitude itself but the apparent size of a
person in pixels, and apparent size scales inversely with altitude. At 50~m the
median person in the drone test set measures 44~px on the square root of box
area, so the same camera at 100~m would place a standing adult near 22~px. That
value falls inside the 17 to 28~px range spanned by the disaster evaluation
sets, whose frames were captured from news helicopters and drones at
operational heights. The corpus therefore covers the pixel-size regime of a
100~m survey through its disaster footage, while the graded 10, 30, and 50~m
flights isolate the effect of scale under otherwise identical camera, scene,
and annotation conditions. Flights at survey altitude with a controlled ground
truth would strengthen this argument further and are left as future work.

Adaptation itself is a supervised fine-tune of the assignment model. The
training mixture holds the full C2A training split together with the real
training frames repeated twenty times, so a few hundred real frames are not
drowned out by the 6{,}135 synthetic images while the synthetic distribution
is retained; 4K frames are downscaled to 1{,}280~px on the long side first.
The fine-tune runs at a reduced learning rate ($5\times10^{-4}$, cosine
schedule) for at most 80 epochs with the same dual early stopping as the main
protocol, and the blend of Equation~\eqref{eq:blend} stays active. The recipe
contains no domain-adaptation machinery beyond data mixing, deliberately: it
measures what a small labelled budget buys with the simplest reliable
procedure, so the result is a floor for more elaborate methods rather than a
claim of novelty in the adaptation itself.

\section{Inference-Time Enhancement: SAHI and TTA}
Beyond the trained architecture, two inference-time procedures are evaluated as
optional operating modes; both leave the trained weights untouched and trade
latency for recall on the smallest targets. Their quantitative effect is
reported in Chapter~IV.

Slicing-aided hyper inference (SAHI) runs the detector over overlapping tiles of
the input so that very small targets occupy a larger fraction of each forward
pass (Figure~\ref{fig:sahipipe}). The image is sliced into square tiles (256 to
640~px, overlap 25--30\%) alongside one full-image pass; the detector runs on
each tile at a low confidence floor, and the per-tile detections are merged by
greedy non-maximum merging with an intersection-over-smaller matching metric at
threshold 0.5, which suits partially-cut boxes at tile borders.

  \caption[Slicing-aided hyper inference.]{Slicing-aided hyper inference:
  overlapping tiles and a full-image pass, detected separately and merged.}
  \label{fig:sahipipe}
\end{figure}

Test-time augmentation (TTA) instead re-runs the detector on rescaled and
flipped copies of the full image, at scale factors 1.0, 0.83, and 0.67, each
with a horizontal flip, and merges the union of predictions with standard NMS
(Figure~\ref{fig:ttapipe}). Because the model is fully convolutional, it can be
evaluated at a higher input resolution than it was trained at; running TTA at
1280~px doubles the effective resolution of the smallest targets. The
exploratory study in Chapter~IV shows the trade-off on an older checkpoint;
the final recommended model was not rerun in that sweep.

The two procedures buy recall in different ways and at different costs. SAHI
multiplies the number of forward passes by the tile count, so its latency grows
with the slicing density; its strength is that each tile is processed at close
to the training resolution, which is what lets it recover the smallest and most
occluded targets along cluttered edges. Test-time augmentation instead runs a
fixed handful of passes at a few scales, so its cost is bounded and predictable,
and its gain comes mainly from the single high-resolution pass rather than from
tiling. Because neither procedure alters the trained weights, both can be
switched on or off at deployment time according to the latency budget an
operator can afford. A ground station reviewing footage after a sortie may
accept SAHI, while a live feed may allow only augmentation or neither. The
archived measurements in Chapter~IV illustrate those costs but do not establish
the final model's operating point.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}
All models were trained and evaluated on a single workstation with an NVIDIA
RTX 4070 Ti SUPER (16~GB, Ada, compute capability 8.9), an Intel Core
i7-14700K, and 128~GB of RAM, running Windows~11. The software stack was
Python~3.11.9, PyTorch~2.12.0 (CUDA~12.6, cuDNN~9.10), and Ultralytics~8.4.56,
with pycocotools for COCO-style evaluation. Training used one GPU with no
distributed data parallelism. Table~\ref{tab:setup} lists the training
configuration, held identical across the four models so that any measured
difference is due to the architecture and not the optimiser.

Two settings deserve their reasoning on record. The optimiser is pinned to
AdamW at $\text{lr}_0 = 0.001$ because the framework's automatic choice
(an SGD variant at $\text{lr}_0 = 0.01$) diverged on the P2-head
architecture, with the validation classification loss growing without bound
near epoch 50; AdamW trained every configuration stably. The early-stopping
patience of 50 epochs, with a separate $F_2$-based stop at 40, was fixed in
the project protocol before the comparative runs. The longer window was used
because preliminary cosine-schedule runs continued to improve late; fixing it
also prevents one architecture from receiving a more favourable stopping rule.

\section{Evaluation Metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}
No single number describes a detector for this task, so four aspects are
measured separately: how reliably the model recovers the people present, how
precisely it localises them, how that performance varies with target size, and
whether its confidence scores and running cost are suitable for deployment.
Each metric is defined below, so that the results reported through the
remainder of this chapter carry an unambiguous meaning.

\subsection{Operational Scores and the Choice of $F_2$}
In search and rescue, a missed person may never get a second look, while a
false alarm costs an operator a few seconds. The primary operating metric is
therefore the $F_2$ score, which weights recall four times as heavily as
precision. With true positives $\mathit{TP}$, false positives $\mathit{FP}$,
and false negatives $\mathit{FN}$, where a detection counts as $\mathit{TP}$
when its intersection over union with a ground-truth box reaches $0.5$
(Equation~\ref{eq:iou}), precision $P$, recall $R$, and the $F_\beta$ family
are
\begin{align}
  P = \frac{\mathit{TP}}{\mathit{TP}+\mathit{FP}}, \qquad
  R = \frac{\mathit{TP}}{\mathit{TP}+\mathit{FN}}, \qquad
  F_\beta = (1+\beta^{2})\,\frac{P \cdot R}{\beta^{2} P + R},
\end{align}
with $\beta = 1$ for the balanced $F_1$ and $\beta = 2$ for $F_2$. Operational
scores are reported at the confidence threshold that maximises each score, and
that threshold is reported too, because a deployed system must commit to one.

\subsection{Localisation Quality and Threshold Selection}
Localisation is measured by COCO average precision~\cite{lin2014coco}:
$\mathrm{AP}$ averaged
over IoU thresholds $0.5$ to $0.95$, together with $\mathrm{AP}_{50}$,
$\mathrm{AP}_{75}$, and the size-partitioned $\mathrm{AP}_S$. For targets a
few pixels wide, $\mathrm{AP}_{50}$ carries most of the signal: a one-pixel
shift on an 8-px box moves its IoU far more than the same shift on an 80-px
box, so strict thresholds are unusually sensitive to both localisation error
and annotation uncertainty at this scale. The aggregate AP is still reported for
comparability, and Section~\ref{sec:quant} shows it is nearly flat across
configurations while $\mathrm{AP}_{50}$ and recall move. Average recall at
100 detections ($\mathrm{AR}_{100}$) is included because C2A scenes carry 20
to 40 people each, so a per-image detection budget of 100 is the realistic
regime.

\subsection{Size-Stratified Recall}
Recall is reported in the size bands used throughout this work (very-tiny
$<8$~px, tiny $8$--$16$~px, small $16$--$32$~px, medium $32$--$96$~px, by
$\sqrt{\text{area}}$). This is the metric that directly tests the P2
hypothesis: if a stride-4 scale helps, the gain must appear in the very-tiny
band, and nowhere else would compensate for its absence.

\subsection{Calibration and Efficiency}
An operator acts on confidence scores, so the scores must mean something. The
expected calibration error (ECE), maximum calibration error, and Brier score
quantify how far predicted confidence sits from empirical accuracy.
Efficiency is reported as parameter count, GFLOPs, and end-to-end latency
(preprocessing, forward pass, and NMS) measured on the 4070 Ti SUPER, because
the deployment claim of this thesis is stated in those units. AP-style
metrics use $\text{conf} = 0.001$ and $\text{IoU} = 0.7$; operational scores
use the per-image protocol at $\text{IoU} = 0.5$.

\section{Dataset}
\label{sec:dataset}
The models are trained and tested on C2A (Combination to Application), a
dataset for human detection in disaster scenes~\cite{nihal2024c2a}. C2A was
chosen because it is the only large-scale benchmark specific to this task:
real search-and-rescue collections exist but are small (about two thousand
frames), which is not enough to train a detector of this size from a
pretrained checkpoint without severe overfitting. C2A is semi-synthetic:
disaster backgrounds from the AIDER aerial-emergency
collection~\cite{kyrkou2020aider} are combined with human poses segmented by
a U$^2$-Net model~\cite{qin2020u2net}, then randomly scaled, placed, and
annotated with horizontal boxes. The dataset contains 10{,}215 images and
roughly 360{,}000 labelled instances across four scene types (collapsed
buildings, fire, flood, and traffic accident), with 20 to 40 people per
image. The composited origin is a stated limitation: results on C2A measure
detection of very small human shapes in disaster clutter. The extended study
in Section~\ref{sec:realresults} measures, rather than assumes, how these
models transfer to fully real imagery.

The frozen split used throughout is given in Table~\ref{tab:split}. Split
hashes are recorded so that the baseline and the three variants all see
exactly the same training, validation, and test images, which is what makes
the single-seed comparison between configurations fair.

\section{\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{Experimentation}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{Implementation}\DIFaddend }
\label{sec:protocol}
The four configurations trained sequentially on the single GPU, one run at a
time, in the ablation order of Table~\ref{tab:variants}. The protocol was
built to survive an unstable power grid and to make every number reproducible.

Before each full run, an automatic smoke test pushed about one percent of the
data through two epochs, exercising every metric writer and plot path; a full
run refused to start unless a smoke pass existed from the previous 24 hours.
Training checkpoints were copied to a safety location every epoch, and a
restarted script resumed from the last checkpoint rather than beginning
again. An out-of-memory retry ladder stepped the batch size down from 16
while gradient accumulation held the effective batch at 16, which is how the
P2-head models ran at a physical batch of 8 without changing the
optimisation. A background sampler logged GPU utilisation, VRAM, power, and
temperature at two-second intervals for the full duration of every run, and
each run wrote an environment manifest (library versions, dataset and weight
hashes, seed, and hyperparameters) so that any number in this chapter can be
traced to the run that produced it.

The four runs took 7.3, 7.0, 10.2, and 29.5 hours (baseline, CBAM, CBAM+P2,
and the state-space variant). The dual early-stopping behaved differently
across them, which is itself informative. The $F_2$ stop ended the CBAM run
after its best score at epoch 239 and the CBAM+P2 run after epoch 218. The
baseline kept improving until epoch 270 and ran out the 300-epoch cap. The
state-space run peaked earliest, at epoch 149, and the fitness patience ended
it at epoch 154; its 29.5-hour wall clock, four times the baseline's, bought
no later improvement. The original ablation chain uses one seed (0), which
limits the strength of small between-model differences. The later assignment
comparison is the only training intervention replicated across three seeds;
Section~\ref{sec:stats} reports those results separately.

\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \subsubsection{\DIFadd{Per-Size Recall}}
\DIFaddend The per-size recall in Table~\ref{tab:persize} explains the aggregate
flatness. The P2 head raises very-tiny ($<8$~px) recall from $0.743$ to
$0.757$ ($+1.5$~points), the band that matters most for spotting distant
victims, but it slightly lowers recall in the tiny, small, and medium bands.
The medium band falls furthest, from $0.909$ to $0.808$, and that direction
deserves an explanation rather than an apology, since a model that improves on
the harder cases might be expected to improve on the easier ones as well.

Three factors account for it. First, the stride-4 level does not add supervision;
it redistributes it. The task-aligned assigner selects a fixed number of positive
samples per ground-truth box, and introducing a fourth and finer level draws
those positives toward cells whose receptive field suits a target a few pixels
across. A 32 to 96~px person covered by that level spans many cells, none of
which sees the whole object, so the supervision it receives is both thinner and
less well matched than at stride 16 or 32. Second, the loss is dominated by the
population it is computed over. Instances below 32~px make up $99.6\%$ of the
test set and a similar share of training, so gradient mass flows almost entirely
toward small targets and the network has little incentive to preserve behaviour
on larger ones. Third, the estimate itself is fragile: the medium band contains
only 317 of roughly $72{,}500$ test instances, $0.44\%$ of the total, and those
317 people appear in just 62 images. In absolute terms the drop is 32 objects,
against 371 additional very-tiny people recovered.

The correct reading is therefore that the P2 head specialises the detector
rather than strictly improving it. Specialisation is a trade, and on this
benchmark the trade is favourable because the population the model gives up is
negligible and the population it gains is the one the application depends on. A
general-purpose detector, evaluated on data with a balanced size distribution,
should not adopt this configuration on the strength of these results. The state-space neck tracks CBAM~+~P2 almost exactly
here, confirming it adds nothing in the size regime it was meant to help; its
scan diagnostics show the module was active (the forward and reverse scans
disagree with a mean cosine distance of $0.836$), so an inactive or disconnected
module does not explain the null result.

\section{Inference-Time Enhancement: SAHI and TTA}
\label{sec:sahitta}
Slicing-aided hyper inference (SAHI)~\cite{akyon2022sahi} runs a detector over
overlapping crops, while test-time augmentation (TTA) evaluates transformed
versions of the full image and merges their predictions. These experiments
predate the final ablation and used the older Mamba+CBAM+P2 checkpoint, not the
recommended CBAM+P2 model. They are retained because they show the operational
trade-off, but they are not evidence for the final model and are excluded from
the primary comparison. The SAHI scores also use per-image matching at
$\mathrm{IoU}=0.5$, so they are not directly comparable with COCO AP.

Table~\ref{tab:sahi} reports the archived sweep. A 512-px slice gives the best
balanced SAHI operating point ($F_2=0.865$, very-tiny recall $0.808$) at
$187$~ms per image. The 256-px setting reaches the highest very-tiny recall
($0.829$) but lowers precision and costs $534$~ms. The separate TTA run at
1280~px increased its checkpoint's $\mathrm{AP}_{50}$ by $1.64$ points and
its COCO AP by $5.39$ points; 1920~px degraded. Because the final CBAM+P2
checkpoint was not rerun under these settings, the study supports only the
general conclusion that higher-resolution inference can exchange latency for
tiny-target recall. It is not included in the thesis headline metrics.

\section{Extended Study: Protocol Hardening and Real-World Adaptation}
\label{sec:partb}
The results so far answer which architectural additions help on the benchmark
as published. A second phase of the work, carried out after the ablation was
complete, asked three harder questions. Is the benchmark itself clean? Once the
architecture search stalled, was there still a lever left for the smallest
targets? And what happens when the trained model meets real footage instead of
composited scenes? This section reports the answers in that order, on the same
workstation and under the same metric definitions as the rest of the chapter;
training settings that differ from Table~\ref{tab:setup} are stated where they
apply. Two findings organise it: training supervision helped after the
architecture search stalled, and adaptation gains were specific to the footage
used for fine-tuning. Figure~\ref{fig:partbchain} summarises the phase as the chain
of decisions the subsections walk through: the re-split is adopted, a
frequency-gated branch is tried and dropped, the assignment blend is kept,
and the real-data fine-tune produces one model evaluated on five held-out
domains.

\subsection{Scene Leakage and the Scene-Disjoint Re-Split}
\label{sec:leak}
C2A composites several synthetic images from each disaster background, and the
official split assigns those images to the training and test sets
independently, so one background scene can appear on both sides. An audit of
the scene identifiers found that 98\% of test-set scenes also occur in the
training set. To measure what that sharing is worth, the dataset was
re-partitioned so that all images of a scene fall on one side (6{,}135
training, 2{,}040 validation, and 2{,}040 test images), and the recommended
model was retrained under the same schedule. Table~\ref{tab:leak} compares the
protocols. The clean split costs $1.6$ points of $\mathrm{AP}_{50}$ and about
one point of very-tiny recall. The leakage therefore inflates the official
numbers only modestly, and no conclusion of the ablation changes, but every
experiment after this point uses the scene-disjoint split so that nothing in
the extended study rides on shared scenes. Its test set holds 72{,}847 person
instances: 24{,}806 below 8~px, 20{,}893 at 8--16~px, 26{,}738 at 16--32~px,
and 410 at 32--96~px.

\subsection{Tiny-Aware Assignment: Sweep and Matched Pair}
\label{sec:assignresults}
Section~\ref{sec:assignmethod} argued that the smallest people are trained on
poorly chosen positive samples because the assigner's IoU term collapses below
16~px, and proposed blending a Wasserstein similarity into it with a weight
$\gamma$. Fifty-epoch pilot runs swept $\gamma$ over $\{0, 0.5, 1.0\}$;
Table~\ref{tab:gamma} and Figure~\ref{fig:gammadose} show a clean
dose--response. Raising $\gamma$ buys very-tiny recall ($0.693$ to $0.712$ to
$0.729$) and sells accuracy on larger targets, slowly at first and then
steeply: the full replacement at $\gamma=1.0$ pays more than five points of
$\mathrm{AP}_S$ for its recall gain, a poor trade, while $\gamma=0.5$ keeps
most of the gain at a tenth of the cost. The half-blend was carried forward.

\subsection{Within-Video Against Cross-Event}
\label{sec:xevent}
The disaster rows of Table~\ref{tab:multidomain} require a distinction between
within-video adaptation and cross-event transfer. The 60 disaster training frames and the 25
R-set evaluation frames come from the same two news videos; the perceptual
hash guarantees no frame is shared, but scenes, lighting, and people overlap.
On that within-video set, adaptation works dramatically. $\mathrm{AP}_{50}$
rises from $0.133$ to $0.411$, recall at the operating threshold from $0.127$
to $0.451$, and recall in the 16--32~px band from $0.230$ to $0.569$. Drone
fine-tuning alone does nothing for the same set ($0.133$ to $0.124$), so the
gain comes from disaster labels specifically, not from real-image exposure in
general.

\subsection{Statistical Validation}
\label{sec:stats}
Several reported differences are small and the real test sets are smaller, so
the key comparisons carry uncertainty estimates. A paired image-level
bootstrap resamples the test images with replacement (1{,}000 resamples),
applies the same resample to both models of a pair, and recomputes each
metric, which yields a 95\% percentile interval and a two-sided $p$-value for
every delta; per-image predictions are cached, so the procedure re-reads
recorded detections rather than re-running inference. This treats test-set
sampling variability, not training-run variability, so the central assignment
claim was additionally replicated: the matched pair of Table~\ref{tab:d2pair}
was retrained twice more, both models at seed 1 and at seed 2, and each pair
was evaluated identically. The very-tiny gain holds in every seed, at
$+2.09$, $+1.66$, and $+1.11$ points (mean $+1.6$, standard deviation
$0.5$), and the blended model's very-tiny recall itself is nearly constant
across seeds ($0.751$, $0.750$, $0.748$). Replication also corrects two
secondary readings rather than defending them. The two-point $\mathrm{AP}_S$
gain of the seed-0 pair does not recur ($+2.0$, $-1.3$, $+0.5$ points,
noise around zero, driven by control-side variance), and the small seed-0
$F_2$ gain averages away ($+0.27$, $-0.11$, $-0.04$). What survives all
three seeds, the bootstrap interval, and the dose--response of
Table~\ref{tab:gamma} is the claim the method was built for: recall of
sub-8-pixel people rises by one to two points at no cost to the overall
operating score.

\section{Conclusion}
The additive ablation shows that the gain on C2A comes almost entirely from
the P2 detection scale, which lifts very-tiny recall and $\mathrm{AP}_{50}$
at a small cost, while CBAM contributes efficiency and the state-space neck
contributes nothing at nearly triple the latency. The recommended CBAM~+~P2
detector reaches $\mathrm{AP}_{50}$ $0.853$ and $F_2$ $0.844$ at $19.6$~M
parameters and $14.6$~ms. Among the detectors in the original C2A benchmark,
only YOLOv9-e scores higher, at roughly three times the parameter count. The
extended study sharpened that picture in three ways. The benchmark's scene
leakage costs $1.6$ points of $\mathrm{AP}_{50}$ and changes no conclusion.
For the smallest targets, label assignment helped where the additional
architecture did not, adding one to two points of sub-8-pixel
recall in every training seed tested, while a frequency-gated branch joined
the state-space neck as a documented negative. And on real footage a labelled
budget of a few hundred frames adapted the model to its target domains at
almost no cost to the source benchmark, with a held-out disaster event
marking where that adaptation stops. Every objective set in Chapter~I is met
by a specific, numbered result in this chapter.

\section{Introduction}
A detector intended for disaster response is used around people at their most
vulnerable. Its errors, data handling, and possible misuse therefore matter
alongside its accuracy. The same person detector could support rescue or
surveillance without any architectural change. This chapter considers that
dual use together with intellectual property, safety, privacy, and the measured
resource cost of the experiments.

\section{Intellectual Property Considerations}
The work builds on external software, data, and published methods. The detector
uses the AGPL-3.0 edition of the Ultralytics YOLO11
framework~\cite{jocher2024yolo11} and PyTorch; CBAM
follows the published design~\cite{woo2018cbam}, and SAHI is used for the
exploratory sliced-inference study~\cite{akyon2022sahi}. A future code or model
release would need to reproduce the exact licences and notices of the versions
used, rather than treating all open-source licences as interchangeable.

The dataset deserves a specific note. C2A is distributed through
Kaggle~\cite{c2akaggle} by its authors, who request citation of the
accompanying paper~\cite{nihal2024c2a} but publish no explicit licence text.
It is therefore used here strictly for academic research, cited in both
forms, and not redistributed. The sources it composites, AIDER
backgrounds~\cite{kyrkou2020aider} and pose imagery segmented with
U$^2$-Net~\cite{qin2020u2net}, carry their own academic-use terms. The
additive ablation design, the trained weights, the negative state-space
finding, and the analysis are the author's contributions; any public release
of weights would state that they derive from C2A and inherit its
academic-use position.

The real footage of the extended study has its own provenance rules. The
drone and campus recordings are the author's own. The disaster frames were
sampled from publicly posted news footage; those frames are copyrighted
material, used here only internally for evaluation and a small fine-tune
for this academic analysis, and they are not redistributed. This statement
describes the handling adopted in the thesis; it is not a general claim that
all such reuse is legally permitted. What the
thesis archives instead is the annotations together with a source manifest
recording each video and the timestamp of every sampled frame, from which
the frames can be regenerated, and each source is attributed.

\section{Ethical Considerations}
The system detects people, which obliges care on several fronts. The first is
dual use: a person detector tuned for rescue is technically indistinguishable
from one used for surveillance. This thesis frames, trains, and evaluates the
model for search and rescue, and Section~\ref{sec:legal5} notes the
regulatory line. Misuse cannot be prevented by the architecture; it depends on
how access and deployment are governed.

The second is error asymmetry. A missed victim is a life risk, while a false
alarm costs an operator seconds, so the evaluation weights recall through the
$F_2$ metric and the operating threshold errs towards flagging, with a human
making every final judgement. Two data-side considerations complete the
picture. The primary training imagery is composited rather than photographic,
so the main development phase used no images of real, identifiable disaster
victims. The extended phase did introduce a small number of real disaster
frames from published news video, because real-world validation cannot be had
without them, and they were handled under three constraints: the material was
already public, no attempt is made to identify any person in it, and the
frames themselves are not redistributed. The author's own drone and campus
recordings were made in open spaces at altitudes where a person spans a few
dozen pixels and is not identifiable. C2A still covers only four scene types
and composited poses, so the model may behave differently on populations,
clothing, or terrain it never saw; the extended study's real-footage results
(Section~\ref{sec:realresults}) are a first measurement of that bias, and
broader multi-event validation remains future work.

\section{Safety Considerations}
The detector is an assistive tool, not an autonomous decision-maker. It
directs an operator's attention and does not clear areas; its outputs must
never be read as a definitive count of survivors, and
Figure~\ref{fig:qualgrid} shows why, since box counts at the review threshold
exceed the annotated count on dense scenes. Safe use also depends on knowing
where the model is weak. The measured failure modes, heavily occluded
targets, few-pixel targets, and merged detections in crowds, are quantified
in Chapter~IV (Table~\ref{tab:persize}, Section~\ref{sec:qual}) so operators
know where trust should thin. For the four C2A configurations, the
$F_2$-optimal confidence lies between 0.16 and 0.20
(Table~\ref{tab:thrcal}) and ECE is near 0.02. These figures support a stable
threshold on that benchmark, but they do not remove the need to validate
calibration after a domain or sensor change.

\section{Legal Considerations}
\label{sec:legal5}
Airborne collection of imagery that contains people is regulated on two
fronts. Drone operation over populated or disaster areas is subject to
civil-aviation permissions on flight, altitude, and no-fly zones; in
Bangladesh these are administered by the Civil Aviation Authority~\cite{caabuav}, and
equivalents apply elsewhere. This thesis concerns the detection model rather
than flight operations, but any deployment inherits those rules. Imagery of
identifiable people also engages data-protection regimes such as the EU
General Data Protection Regulation~\cite{gdpr2016}, even when collected in
an emergency; retention, access, and secondary use of rescue footage need
explicit policy before deployment, and the surveillance-capable nature of
the model makes that policy a requirement rather than a courtesy. Within the
research itself, the main phase used public academic data under the terms
above. The extended phase added the author's own recordings of open public
spaces, in which individuals are not identifiable at the altitudes flown, and
frames from already-public news broadcasts, used for analysis and never
republished; no register of personal data was created.

\section{Impact of the Project on Societal, Health, and Cultural Issues}
Faster location of survivors has a direct health benefit: it shortens the time
to reach injured people, which is decisive in the first hours after an
earthquake or flood. The societal value is greatest where trained search teams
are scarce relative to the affected area, which is the situation in many
disaster-prone regions, including Bangladesh, whose flood seasons regularly
displace communities along major rivers. Because the recommended model is
small (19.6~M parameters, Table~\ref{tab:main}) and runs in real time on a
single consumer GPU, it does not presume expensive infrastructure, which
widens the set of agencies that can afford to operate it. Culturally, aerial
imaging of disaster sites must respect the dignity of victims and local norms
around photographing the dead and injured; the human-in-the-loop design keeps
those judgements with people rather than software.

\section{Impact of Project on the Environment and Sustainability}
The environmental cost of the four original ablation runs was measured. A
tracker recorded energy and estimated emissions, and a two-second sampler
logged GPU power throughout each run; Table~\ref{tab:carbon} reports the
results. The four runs consumed about $10.6$~kWh of GPU energy and correspond
to an estimated $9.0$~kg of CO$_2$ under the configured grid intensity. Two
design choices kept the
figure low: saved weights were re-evaluated instead of retrained whenever a
metric could be recomputed, and the ablation ran one seed per configuration
in this phase. The state-space variant is the environmental outlier as well
as the accuracy one: it produced more than half of the total emissions
($4.65$~kg, $2.6$ times the recommended model's) for no measured gain, which
turns the argument for honest negative results into an environmental one.
Energy-aware reporting of this kind follows the practice argued for in the
literature on the carbon cost of deep learning~\cite{strubell2019energy}, and
the tracking used the CodeCarbon library~\cite{codecarbon}. The extended
study added roughly 120 GPU-hours across its re-baseline, assignment,
adaptation, and replication runs, an estimated further $20$~kWh at the same
board powers; the estimate is stated rather than tracker-measured because
those runs executed on shared laboratory machines. At inference, the
recommended model's $14.6$~ms latency on a consumer GPU translates to a small
per-image energy cost, and choosing the 19.6~M-parameter design over a model
three times larger (Table~\ref{tab:sota}) reduces the energy drawn by every
future deployment of the system.

\section{Conclusion}
These considerations affected the engineering choices. The recall-weighted
evaluation represents the task's asymmetric error cost, the
composited dataset avoids imagery of real victims, the documented failure
modes and stable operating point make the human-in-the-loop role practical,
and the measured carbon account puts a number on what each architectural
decision cost the environment. The dual-use nature of person detection
remains the standing obligation: the model built here to find people in
rubble must be governed, wherever it is deployed, so that it stays on that
side of the line.

\section{Introduction}
The International Engineering Alliance's graduate-attribute
framework~\cite{iea2021gapc} defines the
attributes of a Complex Engineering Problem (WP1 to WP7) and of Complex
Engineering Activities (EA1 to EA5). A problem qualifies as complex when it
demands in-depth knowledge (WP1) together with some or all of the remaining
attributes; an activity qualifies when its execution shows the corresponding
range of resources, interactions, and innovation. This chapter maps the
thesis onto those attributes, and each claim points at the section, table,
or figure of this report where the evidence sits.

\section{Complex Engineering Problems Associated with the Current Thesis}

Seven attributes mark a problem as one of complex engineering. Each is taken in
turn below and matched against what this thesis actually required, from the depth
of background knowledge needed to the conflicting requirements that had to be
traded off. Every claim points at the section, table, or figure that carries its
evidence, so that the mapping can be checked rather than taken on trust.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{WP1: Depth of knowledge required.} The default detector was
        inadequate for the sub-ten-pixel regime measured in this dataset. The
        investigation required working knowledge of one-stage detection
        architectures at the layer level (Table~\ref{tab:layers}), of
        channel and spatial attention (Section~\ref{sec:cbam3}), of selective
        state-space models and their scan mechanics (Section~\ref{sec:mamba}), and of
        the loss geometry that makes few-pixel boxes unstable under strict
        IoU (Sections~\ref{sec:loss3} and~\ref{sec:metrics}), a fundamentals-first analysis rather
        than tool use.
  \item \textbf{WP2: Range of conflicting requirements.} The core tension is
        technical and non-technical at once: recall on the smallest targets
        against a parameter and latency budget for airborne hardware, and
        the humanitarian value of the system against its surveillance
        potential (Chapter~V). The resolution is measured, not asserted: the
        P2 scale buys very-tiny recall at $+0.5$~M parameters and $+1$~ms
        (Table~\ref{tab:main}), and the recall-weighted $F_2$ metric encodes
        the ethical asymmetry directly into the evaluation.
  \item \textbf{WP3: Depth of analysis, no obvious solution.} Which of
        attention, resolution, or sequence modelling helps tiny-target
        detection had no a-priori answer; each had a plausible mechanism. The
        additive ablation (Section~\ref{sec:protocol}) provides the comparison,
        and its result distinguishes one useful scale change, one efficient
        substitution, and one costly null result.
  \item \textbf{WP4: Familiarity of issues.} The dominant issues are
        infrequently encountered in standard detection practice: targets
        smaller than one detection cell (Figure~\ref{fig:strideprob}), a
        dataset whose instances are $99.6\%$ below 32~px
        (Figure~\ref{fig:sizedist}), and a training collapse specific to
        the state-space blocks at epoch 28 (Figure~\ref{fig:training}).
  \item \textbf{WP5: Beyond standards and codes.} No engineering standard
        prescribes how to evaluate or deploy a tiny-human detector for
        search and rescue. The evaluation protocol, the frozen-split and
        provenance rules, and the operational threshold policy
        (Sections~\ref{sec:metrics} and~\ref{sec:protocol}) had to be designed for the problem.
  \item \textbf{WP6: Stakeholder scope.} The stakeholders of a rescue
        detector, operators who act on its boxes, agencies bound by aviation
        and data-protection rules, and the people being imaged, have needs
        that pull in different directions; Chapter~V works through those
        obligations and the design choices that answer them.
  \item \textbf{WP7: Interdependence.} The system is a chain of coupled
        sub-problems: dataset properties drive the architecture
        (Section~\ref{sec:p2novel}), the architecture drives memory behaviour and hence
        the training configuration (Section~\ref{sec:setup}), and the evaluation design
        determines whether any of it can be attributed. The single-variable
        ablation exists precisely to cut through that interdependence.
\end{itemize}

\section{Complex Engineering Activities Associated with the Current Thesis}

Where the previous section described the nature of the problem, this one
describes what carrying out the work involved. Five attributes define a complex
engineering activity, and each is set against the resources the project drew on,
the way the experiments were organised, and the judgements that had to be made
with incomplete information. The same referencing convention applies.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{EA1: Range of resources.} The work managed diverse
        resources: a 10{,}215-image benchmark with frozen splits, pretrained
        weights, a single 16~GB consumer GPU shared across 54 hours of
        training (Table~\ref{tab:carbon}), open-source frameworks, and a
        metric pipeline producing per-run manifests, CSV data, and plots so
        that every reported number is regenerable.
  \item \textbf{EA2: Level of interactions.} Theoretical design met hardware
        limits directly. The stride-4 scale quadruples head activation
        memory, which forced the physical batch to 8 with gradient
        accumulation holding the effective batch at 16 (Section~\ref{sec:setup}), and
        the automatic optimiser choice diverged on that same architecture,
        which forced the pinned AdamW configuration; resolving these
        interactions without breaking comparability was the day-to-day
        engineering of the thesis.
  \item \textbf{EA3: Innovation.} The individual modules are established; the
        contribution lies in how they are tested and selected. Attention is
        substituted rather than appended, the state-space blocks are isolated
        in the neck of an otherwise unchanged detector, and tiny-object recall
        and $F_2$ are treated as decision metrics. The later assignment blend
        then modifies positive-sample selection without adding inference cost
        (Section~\ref{sec:assignmethod}).
  \item \textbf{EA4: Consequences for society and the environment.} The
        activity was run with its externalities measured: a carbon tracker
        and power sampler on every run (9.0~kg CO$_2$ total,
        Table~\ref{tab:carbon}), an ethical position on dual use and
        training-data dignity (Chapter~V), and a deliverable whose small
        size lowers the energy cost of every future deployment.
  \item \textbf{EA5: Familiarity, extension beyond prior experience.} The
        project extended well past coursework: reading and adapting current
        detection, attention, and state-space literature; diagnosing a
        silent model-rebuild fault that had invalidated an earlier line of
        results; and building failure-tolerant training infrastructure
        (smoke tests, resume, out-of-memory ladders) for a load-shedding
        environment, none of which is met in standard undergraduate
        laboratory work. The extended phase (Section~\ref{sec:partb}) added
        two more activities of this kind: checkpoint-resumable training
        chains run unattended on shared laboratory machines, and a
        data-engineering pipeline for the real-imagery sets, from frame
        extraction and perceptual-hash de-duplication through assisted
        annotation and automated quality audits
        (Section~\ref{sec:adaptmethod}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Limitations}

Several of the limits below were measured rather than merely acknowledged, and
that distinction matters. Where the extended phase could quantify a weakness it
did so, even when the resulting number is unflattering: the synthetic-to-real
gap and the failure below eight pixels are both stated with the figures attached.
The remaining limits concern the size of the real evaluation sets and the single
seed behind the original ablation, which bound how firmly the smaller
differences can be read. They are set out here so the results are taken at their
proper weight, and each points to the work in Section~\ref{sec:future} that would
close it.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Semi-synthetic primary benchmark.} The main results are on
        C2A, whose scenes composite real backgrounds with segmented human
        figures, and whose paste-based annotations cap localisation accuracy
        for every model compared. The extended phase measured the transfer
        gap directly instead of leaving it open, and the gap is large:
        zero-shot $\mathrm{AP}_{50}$ falls from $0.844$ on C2A to $0.335$ on
        the drone test and $0.133$ on real disaster frames.
  \item \textbf{Small real evaluation sets.} The real test sets hold 23 to
        60 frames each, so their scores carry wide intervals and should be
        read as evidence of direction, not precise levels. The within-video
        disaster result also shares source videos between training and
        evaluation frames, deliberately and with de-duplication; the honest
        cross-event number is the held-out one, and it is not significant.
  \item \textbf{Sub-8-pixel people in real footage remain undetected.} On
        the disaster R-set, recall below 8~px is zero for every model
        tested, across all 54 such instances. The regime the thesis targets
        on synthetic data is still open on real data, and the adaptation
        fine-tune also paid 13 points of precision on the held-out event.
  \item \textbf{Single seed for most training runs.} The ablation and the
        adaptation runs use seed 0, and the bootstrap intervals of
        Section~\ref{sec:stats} treat test-set variation only. The central
        assignment pair is the exception: replicated at three seeds, its
        very-tiny gain held in all three while the seed-0 $\mathrm{AP}_S$
        gain did not, which calibrates how far any other single-seed
        difference in this thesis should be trusted.
  \item \textbf{Sparse medium-target evidence.} The medium band holds only
        317 of roughly 72{,}500 test instances on the official split (410 on
        the scene-disjoint split), so its recall estimates are noisy.
  \item \textbf{No on-device measurement.} Latency was measured on a desktop
        GPU. Embedded or airborne hardware will behave differently, and no
        field trial with a drone was performed.
\end{itemize}

\section{Recommendations for Future Works}
\label{sec:future}
The limitations above set the agenda, and the extension phase has already
converted three earlier recommendations into results: the enhanced dataset
with the author's own imagery now exists as the real-imagery suite of
Table~\ref{tab:owndata}, its adaptation study is reported in
Section~\ref{sec:realresults}, and the multi-seed replication of the central
comparison is reported in Section~\ref{sec:stats}. What follows is what
remains.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Multi-event disaster data.} The cross-event result shows
        that 60 frames from two events adapt the model to those events, not
        to the disaster domain. A disaster training set spanning many
        events, rather than many frames per event, is the most direct route
        to generalisation, and the annotation pipeline built here makes each
        added event cheap.
  \item \textbf{Server-side deployment.} Work is under way to serve the
        recommended model behind a streaming pipeline in which a drone
        transmits footage to a ground server that runs detection and returns
        flagged frames to the operator. This keeps the airborne payload
        light, allows the $1280$-px test-time augmentation mode when the
        link budget permits, and is the practical route to field use.
  \item \textbf{Composing the levers.} The assignment blend, the P2 scale,
        and inference-time slicing all target the same sub-8-pixel band by
        different mechanisms; whether they stack on real footage, where that
        band is still lost entirely, is an open and testable question.
  \item \textbf{Validation on public rescue imagery.} Zero-shot and
        fine-tuned evaluation on collections such as
        SARD~\cite{sambolek2021sard}, HERIDAL~\cite{bozic2019heridal},
        VisDrone~\cite{zhu2021visdrone}, and
        Okutama-Action~\cite{barekatain2017okutama} would ground the
        generalisation claim beyond the author's own sets.
  \item \textbf{A purpose-built state-space design.} Two rejected branches
        do not close the architecture direction: a neck designed around the
        geometry of very small targets, rather than a generic scan or a
        generic frequency gate, remains worth investigating, now with a
        validated protocol and a statistical harness to test it against.
\end{itemize}

Table~\ref{tab:apphistory} is the second record: every architectural variant
tried after the baseline was fixed, with the outcome it produced and the
decision it drove. It is kept so that the negative results carry the same weight

state-space neck was attempted twice: the first run was silently invalidated by

part of a project that a results chapter usually omits.

Finally, a note on reproducibility. Every run recorded the artifacts needed to
repeat it. Training used a single fixed seed (0) with deterministic cuDNN
kernels; the train, validation, and test splits are pinned by content hash so
that no image crosses between them; and each run wrote an environment manifest
with the library versions, the dataset and weight-file hashes, and the full
hyperparameter set. The body's final ablation ran on the RTX~4070~Ti~SUPER of
Section~\ref{sec:setup}, whereas the family benchmark of
Table~\ref{tab:appfamily} predates that machine and used the shorter protocol
noted above, which is why its absolute scores are offset from the body's. These
records let the negative results here be reported as measurements, not
impressions.
